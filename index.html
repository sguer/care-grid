Reimagining Resilience

In 2021, my mother, a nurse clinician, opened a seniors' residence during COVID-19 lockdowns. It was her dream, and I led the finance and operations. Because she prioritized high-quality care above all else, our margins were non-existent. The goal was never profit, but it meant we had no wiggle room to even begin putting aside money to acquire the property while we were also holding other jobs. When the owner sold the building in 2022, we didn't have the capital to relocate. My mom spent the following year ensuring every resident found a new home and I worked to ensure every liability was cleared.

What I find so relevant to foresight and futures thinking is that I had my eye on everything: finances, information systems, and operational metrics. Yet, I completely bypassed my own embodied experience. The cost I didn't track was my own health, which declined sharply inside that work. No system, including my own, detected the toll until after the doors were closed.

The grief was heavy because this was also a personal dream of community. My paternal grandmother was active there and found ways to endear herself to everyone despite the language barrier. I had started dreaming about a space where she could belong as her capacities changed. I pictured an extension of home where my parents could eventually age with dignity and cultural safety.

I've only recently started speaking about this through a lens of transformation rather than failure. It marked the moment I realized that when proximity care isn't set up to tend to the people providing it, the extra energy required to build resilience has to come from somewhere. Usually, it comes directly out of the people holding the community together.

Recipes of Memories: Before, During, After the Smoke

Andy Sabrina Guerrier (she/her/elle) — Fellow, Brain Climate & Equity Collaborative

A Note on Land, Water and Foodways

I write from Turtle Island, or so-called Canada, on the traditional and unsurrendered territory of the Anishinaabe Algonquin Nation. Within earshot of the Kichi Sibi — the Ottawa River — pathways wind through the oaks, carrying their own currents: cyclists, walkers, runners, all converging toward the water.

The river's voice meets these shifting human rhythms, carrying the long memory of the Land and drawing my ancestral roots into the cadence of my days. The Latibonit flows through the stories my father shared: the fierce and silted river whose current ran wide through the Artibonite Valley in Haiti. The Taíno, original stewards of the Caribbean islands, knew it as Ja'tibonicu, the Great High Place of the Sacred Waters.

In the sediment of these revered waters, the mazonbèl took to the soil of Hispaniola through forced dispossession and displacement. Preserved as a memory crop in the grounds where African ancestors carved out survival, this tuber naturalized into Indigenous Caribbean and Haitian foodways as a practice of subsistence.

Yet this lineage of resilience now meets a changing climate. Caught between the memory of abundance and the stresses of a warming world, the mazonbèl faces a dual threat: the salt of rising tides and the spread of taro leaf blight, a destructive pathogen that collapses whole harvests.

What follows are three recipes held in memory across the wildfire seasons: the calm before the smoke, the heavy air during those days of poor air quality, and the slow recovery that comes after. I have felt the weight of those seasons in the sealed air of my home, where familiar foodways offer a small sanctuary. Peeling the rough skin of a mazonbèl alongside my grandmother, Granma, reminds me that routine can be a steadying force in uncertain times.

A resilient kitchen carries warm knowledge that nourishes us not just in body, but in the breath we reclaim with every shared recipe.

I. Mazonbèl Bouyi ak Sòs Pwa Nwa — Before

For the days when Granma makes her slow loop around the neighbourhood, sometimes wandering a bit farther. The air quality index sits comfortably low, letting each unhurried breath carry her forward.

Setup

Portable Induction Cooktop
A surface with LED display (300–1800W). With an extension cord, it travels to the sunroom or the backyard when conditions permit.
A heavy-duty extension cord
Provides the reach to prioritize natural ventilation.
Two Heavy-Bottomed Ferrous Pots
Essential for induction. With tight-fitting lids, they hold heat long after the power is cut.

Ingredients

  • 2–3 mazonbèl tubers, peeled under running water
  • Water, enough to cover by two fingers
  • 1 cup dry black beans
  • 2 tablespoons of épis (If the jar is running low, stick to one tablespoon)
  • A sprig of thyme and a whole scotch bonnet poked with two cloves
  • Small handful of fresh parsley, stems and leaves
  • 2 tablespoons white vinegar
  • 3 tablespoons olive oil
  • Salt, a cautious pinch (Granma has high blood pressure)

Method

  1. Peel the roots in large, confident strokes.

    Any worry about not doing it the way Manman does can wait. The skin comes off easier when the tuber is firm and fresh.

  2. Cook the beans.

    In a heavy pot, boil the dry beans in plenty of water. Whenever they reach a hard boil, add a cup of cold water to shock the skins and help them soften. Repeat this until the beans are tender and the liquid is dark and inky. Mash a handful of the softened beans through a strainer into a paste to thicken the base.

  3. Boil the mazonbèl.

    While the beans cook, place the tubers in the second pot with the thyme and salted water. Set the portable induction cooktop to High just until the first bubbles break the surface. Once it boils, cover the pot with a tight lid and drop the setting to a Low Simmer. Set the LED display for 30 minutes. By using the lid and a lower wattage, the steam stays in to soften the mazonbèl tubers, and Granma does not have to stand over the pot. They are ready when a fork slides through without resistance.

  4. Make the sòs pwa nwa.

    While the mazonbèl finishes, heat olive oil in a separate heavy pot. Soften a few slices of onion, add the épis, and stir until it is fragrant. Add the cooked beans along with their liquid. Nestle the whole scotch bonnet on top. Let it simmer low, lid slightly open, until the sauce thickens.

  5. Serve.

    Drain the mazonbèl tubers. Slice them into rounds, then arrange them on a plate the way Granma likes it — overlapping like a hand of cards. Ladle the sauce on it until she tells you to stop; she usually is generous.

Yield: A meal of unhurried presence.

II. Mazonbèl ak Chiktay Aransò — During the Smoke

For the days when the windows are sealed. The smoke tightens like a tourniquet, cinching homes, lungs, and minds in its now-familiar grip. Each breath wrenched free becomes a rationed triumph. Granma remains indoors.

Ingredients

  • 3 medium Mazonbèl tubers (If the supply from the Caribbean grocery has been uneven, you may already have taro from the Filipino or African markets set aside for days like this.)
  • ½ cup Chiktay Aransò (Spiced Smoked Herring), prepared in advance. A batch has been sitting in a jar in the fridge, the briny flavors mingling into the kind of taste that might wake the appetite. If not for the silent infiltration of smoke.
  • Water, enough to cover by two fingers.

Method

Boil the mazonbèl in a tightly covered pot until fork tender. If using malanga or other taro-like tubers, simmer gently; they soften faster than mazonbèl.

Do not add salt to the boiling water — the chiktay will carry the salt and seasoning.

The aransò topping was made weeks ago by Manman and Granma, back when you all had the time and foresight. Now the house is sealed, the filters overloaded, and reheating anything too fragrant would be overwhelming.

Let the steam do the work. Once the mazonbèl is cooked, spoon the chilled chiktay over the hot pieces and cover the bowl. The rising heat warms everything gently.

Serve.

A portable air purifier runs in the sunroom, a low hum that has become a metronome to the laboured breathing of both Granma and you. Wildfire smoke from fires burning far north has settled over the city. You can taste metal at the back of your throat, shaking your inhaler too many times to count since the skies turned copper.

Yield: A meal of preservation.

III. Té Kanèl — After the Smoke

When the smoke clears and the lungs remember how to expand. Leftovers from the hazy days carry us forward. Fog settled on our brains, not lifted so much as slowly loosening its hold through the slow recovery.

Ingredients

  • 2 sticks of cinnamon (Kanèl) and a piece of star anise
  • A stalk of citronella (lemongrass)
  • Sugar, honey, or syrup — kept on the table, not in the pot. Granma has diabetes; everyone sweetens their own cup.
  • A grieving poem, to be whispered while the water boils:

    mes mains sont racines,
    je tiens les souvenirs
    déterrés pour subsister,
    là où l'air nous terre

Instructions

  1. Boil the cinnamon and anise until the water turns amber.
  2. Serve hot with whatever leftovers carried you through the smoke.
  3. Open the windows. The house laughs, and the rising steam announces the comforting presence of air that no longer needs to be feared. Until the next shift in the air.

Granma eats slowly, her breath still careful, but she is here. We are here. For now, that is enough.

Yield: A cup of renewal.

Polyvocality, or How Stories of Change Become Choruses

Preface

A well-known fable of risk perception is the canary in the coal mine: a small bird carried underground because carbon monoxide would affect it before it incapacitated the miners. Beyond this narrative of early warning of impending human risk, shared vigilance runs through the fabric of collective life. Among social species such as meerkats, babblers, birds, and ungulates, attention is distributed toward the edges of the group, so that a change detected at the periphery becomes a signal the whole group answers. A sentinel vocalizes an approaching threat; the others orient, gather, or flee.

Human early-warning systems aim at this same sequence of monitoring, detecting and alerting, and in that sense they inherit the sentinel's function. But they reproduce detection and leave out relation. Continuous, real-time monitoring produces a signal; a warning becomes real when people find their own account of the danger inside it—when the warning tells a story they recognise as theirs, a convergence that raises the question of narrative authority: whose account of vulnerability shapes what counts as danger, whose exposure demands a response, and which forms of protection are authorized? Shifting this authority requires warning systems designed to integrate community knowledge, surface overlooked vulnerabilities, and enable people to co-author the interventions that protect their health and livelihoods.

From Birdsong to Polyvocality

Tell me, who keeps watch for weary birds?

Before the forest cracked and wheezed, a bird sang until it could not, and those who heard its silence fled.

Through the haze, another bird called not in warning but in summons—who is here?—and those who rowed the air gathered.

Listen to the sky inhaling smoke, the flock binding siren to song: only a chorus, only a chorus of wings can both warn and shelter with its wind.

To build a chorus is to engage in polyvocality: the practice of keeping different situated accounts in relation, so that the meaning of a threat is not fixed by a single authority but produced through the relation between accounts. Rather than dressing a moral lesson in the costume of an animal fable, the call to bind siren to song encodes a relationship between signal, interpretation, and response.

who is here?

Voice does not rely on physical hearing, nor is it a solitary act of expression. As a situated relation, voice arises in call-and-response: Collins describes this Black discourse mode as one in which, "for ideas to be tested and validated, everyone in the group must participate" (Collins, 2000, p. 261). Silence, then, may mark movement rather than disappearance (cf. Collins, 2000, p. 98). Tell me, who keeps watch for weary birds? narrates a bird’s silence as a warning, but calls a chorus of wings to take up the watch in turn. If voice is participation rather than sound, then the weary sentinel’s silence is not so much a discontinuity as a shift in who is watching. In a chorus of voices, yielding to rest welcomes the next speaker, because building a shared account of change depends on the alternation of speaking and listening. No one can hold the watch indefinitely, and the duty to rest upholds the duty to maintain a relational infrastructure for those who share a vulnerability they recognize as constitutive of interdependence rather than as an individual deficit. The meaning of a change comes to exist in that yielding and relating—the very dialogue that story stewardship protects.

From Lone Sentinel to Chorusing Flock

Storytelling has long been a means of shaping meaning, memory, and collective action. Now the attention economy is being challenged by an emerging AI-mediated trust economy, in which autonomous agents increasingly search, evaluate, recommend, negotiate, and act on behalf of others. Is a synthetic recommendation a voice borrowed? A story may matter less for the attention it wins than for the range of futures it makes credible, and thus for the probabilities that people and machines assign to those futures.

Once stories shape which futures count as plausible, the question becomes who may decide what counts as relevant, credible, urgent, or actionable. Narrative authority names that power: the authority to shape the expectations through which people and machines interpret what comes next.

Wings in Chorus, Flock in Cypher

We, who tell stories of how canaries in coal mines warn of unseen dangers, know the air that traps sentinels below and above ground. Sentinels’ songs bear witness to how unequally exposure to harm is distributed, and to the shared vulnerability among those who breathe the same air.

In “Caged Bird,” Maya Angelou writes of clipped wings and bound feet, and still the bird’s throat opens to sing. The caged canary, cast as a sentinel with the coal mine as the stage of its fate, had song and breath held captive by toxic air. What the two birdsongs share is the spatial reality of a confined space: a song one would expect to match the expansiveness of the skies first meets the bars of a cage or the invisible clutches of poisonous air. Immobilized bodies are not thereby silenced; the testimonies carried in song can cross time and space when voices are couples to choruses.

In many African and Afro-diasporic musical traditions, the chorus may answer a solo, start before the soloist has finished,sustain a refrain while another voice improvises, or layer voices in different rhythmic positions. Under chattel slavery, slave codes banned drums and horns to repress the capacity of enslaved people to communicate, organize, and sustain a collective life. The 1688 Slave Code of Barbados, for example, penalized owners who did not search enslaved people's houses weekly and burn any "Drums, Horns, or other loud Instruments." South Carolina's slave code used the same wording, "the using or keeping of drums, horns, or other loud instruments" (Sullivan, 2019; Jennings, 2016). The planters justified the ban by pointing to the threat of uprisings, and the ban served retribution and prevention at once.

Narratives of Black communal life as incipient threat to slavery reveal the power of the prohibited instrument of storytelling: the drum was outlawed for its capacity to assemble dispersed kin and harmonize clandestine communication with community life. And yet, through rhythm fostered by hands and feet in lieu of drums, and movement choreographed to resemble worship rather than prohibited dancing, enslaved communities met in praise houses, homes, and barns, to form ring shouts.

The collective practice blended ancestral memory with the need for mediums to re-story what had to be encoded: tacit knowledge, what could not be said aloud, the names that had no public record, and the grief that had no sanctioned place. Here, re-storying meant relocating storytelling into renewed forms of belonging shown through how people gathered rather than what they said . Africans displaced by the Atlantic slave trade and dispossessed of their instruments, their languages, and their public rites turned to body percussion—hand claps, foot stomps, shuffling steps—and small instruments like bones and tambourines to take on the resonance of prohibited drums.

With a counter-clockwise formation of shufflers serving as a stage of musical resistance, the roles and scripts of the ring shouts were brought to life through alternation between caller and chorus: a songster called out the leading line, the basers as the responding chorus answered with overlapping counter-refrains, and a stick-man struck the wooden floor (Rosenbaum, 2017). Shouters keep their feet low by gliding them in a shuffle instead of lifting them into a dance step.

Rhythmic storytelling moves along a continuum from cipher to cypher: a cipher encodes what cannot safely be said; a cypher gathers voices to receive, answer, and retell it. In each, narrative transforms isolated knowledge into a collective account capable of warning, mourning, and mobilizing.

Chorus as Algorithm

Dèyè mòn, gen mòn
Behind the mountain, there are more mountains.
— Haitian proverb

Through whose memories of mountains already travelled do we sense the mountains ahead? Stories, pathways, and judgements carried forward by those who came before are initial coordinates that orient us long before we take a step.

Optimization models this predicament by treating decision-making as terrain. In a complex system with many possible configurations, every option sits beside the choices that differ from it by a single small change. When each choice is scored according to how well it satisfies target conditions, those scores form elevation: a hill becomes a cluster of neighbouring choices whose scores rise toward a local best, and the top of the hill is a decision that no single immediate tweak can improve. Hill-climbing search moves across this terrain step by step, evaluating adjacent choices and continuing as long as the next move improves the score.

Yet what has the search found when every step from its peak leads downhill: the highest point of the whole landscape, or only the highest point accessible from where it was initialized? The search algorithm stops there because its only rule is to seek immediate local improvement. Unable to register what lies beyond its immediate neighbours, it cannot distinguish a local peak from a global summit. Had it initialized somewhere else—guided by different memories, assumptions, or priorities—it might have stopped on a completely different peak. The starting position decides where the search ends, reporting a local high point as the overall answer simply because of where the journey began.

The chorus offers a way to rethink what it might mean to initialize a search from more than one position at once. Where hill-climbing selects a starting point and reports the peak that follows from it, the chorus holds several starting points in relation. Each voice begins from inherited coordinates, allowing differences among their resulting peaks to become evidence rather than noise. Mégevand characterizes the chorus as dynamic, unstable, and mobile. Possibilities emerge from that instability: new identities can be assumed, interventions can take different forms across the course of a play, transitions can be announced, and linear action can be disrupted. Such instability makes the chorus an analogy rather than a method. No chorus literally performs hill-climbing, yet its polyvocality raises a question: what becomes difficult to perceive when a search begins from only one position? Algorithms and humans share the necessity of starting somewhere, along with the impossibility of surveying an entire landscape from a single point of departure.

Why It Matters for a Theory of Change

Programs and policies can mistake reaching a local target metric for solving a problem. They stop at the first local peak, unaware that their initial baseline left some people's vulnerabilities and alternative horizons completely out of view.

The shift is from single-origin optimization to polyvocal initialization and narrative accountability:

  • Ask whose prior knowledge sets the coordinates.
  • Deliberately seed the search from multiple communities' starting points.
  • Compare the peaks reached.
  • Treat what remains out of view not as nonexistent, but as a claim to be surfaced, contested, and owned by those who live there.
  • Hold ancestral wisdom as a deep memory of terrain, but as one chorus among many, not as the whole landscape.
  • Practise polyvocality: multiple starting positions, varied vantages, and choruses of lived experience that can name both the mountain ahead and the mountains behind it.

What Polyvocality Changes

Polyvocality changes what a result is expected to mean. Dèyè mòn, gen mòn: no peak is the last mountain. In practice, that means:

  • Keep asking whose starting point set the coordinates: whose mountains the evidence base remembers, and whose it leaves out.
  • Differences between peaks become evidence. When multiple starting points reach different peaks, the differences are not noise to be averaged away. They are information about the landscape: what each starting position prioritized, what trade-offs the scoring function hides, what each peak leaves out. A single peak reports “this is best.” Multiple peaks report “best depends on where you began and what you were measuring.”
  • When a target is met, attribute the result: for whom was it met? Disclose the boundary of measurement: whom did the indicator leave out? And bring those left out into the next steps—reviewing the data, redefining the indicator, and choosing where the next search starts. Who decides whether this result counts as the target being met is integral to that first question.
  • Different voices can change what counts as higher. The elevation function, the score, is not neutral. It encodes whose values define improvement. A shorter route may be less safe. A timetable with fewer clashes may concentrate inconvenience on particular students. Polyvocality means contesting the elevation function itself, not only the starting point.
  • Who decided higher means better? Strict hill-climbing permits only moves that yield immediate improvement, leaving little room for descent. Polyvocality accepts descent, treating it not as failure or regression but as a necessary re-initialization. A step down from a local peak need not signal failure or regression; it can be a deliberate reorientation into terrain inaccessible from the summit. Alternate routes, different measures of value, and previously obscured possibilities often become visible only after leaving the height one has reached.

On the meaning of Outcomes

To speak of outcomes in polyvocal terms is to ask not only whether a target has been met, but who has been positioned to define its meaning, asking whose account of the climb is carried forward and whose is treated as background.

For the emblematic canary in the coal mine enshrines in a single image the relation between breath and warning, between vulnerability and knowledge, while the chorus offers something else: a form in relation in which an outcome can be recognized as belonging to those who call and respond.

Outcomes, then, become relational rather than terminal. Because a theory of change that can hold only a single voice will produce single-peak outcomes—calling the local summit a global one. What a chorus invites instead is a theory of change that treats outcome as an ongoing relation among voices and silences.
